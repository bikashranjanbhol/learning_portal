AI answer engines absorb a meaningful share of short informational queries — the
"what is X" traffic — while leaving traffic for depth, worked examples and
original material largely intact. The practical consequence for technical content
is a change in _which_ pages earn visits, not a uniform decline across all of them.

This is worth getting right, because the two obvious reactions — panic and denial
— both lead somewhere expensive.

## What kind of query actually gets absorbed?

The ones an LLM can answer completely from training data, where the reader wants
a fact rather than an understanding.

| Query type                                              | Absorbed by AI answers? |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| "what is consistent hashing"                            | Largely yes             |
| "python dict comprehension syntax"                      | Almost entirely         |
| "consistent hashing vs rendezvous, when to use which"   | Partly                  |
| "why did our cache miss rate spike after adding a node" | Rarely                  |
| Anything requiring your own benchmarks                  | No                      |

The pattern: the more a query needs synthesis of _your_ specific material —
numbers you measured, diagrams you drew, interview data you collected — the less
an answer engine can satisfy it without sending the reader to you.

<Callout variant="note" title="This is a shift in mix, not just volume">
  A site whose traffic is 90% definitional queries is in real trouble. A site whose traffic is
  mostly "how do I actually do this, with the trade-offs" is affected much less. Look at your
  query mix before deciding how worried to be.
</Callout>

## What should you do differently?

Three things, in order of leverage.

**Publish material that cannot be synthesised.** Your own benchmarks, your own
failure post-mortems, dated specifics. An answer engine can paraphrase a
definition of consistent hashing from a thousand sources; it cannot invent the
latency numbers you measured on your own cluster last month.

**Answer the question in the first 60 words.** Counterintuitively, being the
source that gets extracted is good — citations are how discovery works now. The
page that buries its answer under 800 words of preamble is neither cited nor read.

**Decide deliberately about crawlers.** Blocking `GPTBot`, `ClaudeBot` and
`PerplexityBot` everywhere costs you citations and discovery. Allowing them
everywhere gives away paid content. The reasonable middle is allowing them on
free material and disallowing them on premium paths:

```ts
// app/robots.ts
export default function robots(): MetadataRoute.Robots {
  return {
    rules: [
      { userAgent: '*', allow: '/', disallow: ['/api/', '/dashboard/', '/settings/'] },
      {
        userAgent: ['GPTBot', 'ClaudeBot', 'PerplexityBot'],
        allow: ['/blog/', '/courses/'],
        disallow: ['/learn/', '/api/', '/dashboard/', '/settings/'],
      },
    ],
    sitemap: 'https://example.com/sitemap.xml',
  };
}
```

## What should you measure?

Most people are not measuring this at all, which means they are guessing about a
channel that is already material. Segment referrals by source from day one:

1. Traffic with referrers from `chatgpt.com`, `perplexity.ai`, `claude.ai`
2. Organic search traffic, split by query intent where you can
3. The ratio between them, tracked over months rather than weeks

<Callout variant="warning" title="The honest caveat">
  Referral data from AI assistants is incomplete — many sessions arrive without a referrer at
  all. Treat the numbers as a lower bound and a trend line, not a measurement. Anyone quoting a
  precise percentage for this is overstating what the data supports.
</Callout>

## The uncomfortable conclusion

If organic search is your only acquisition channel, this is a structural risk
regardless of how the numbers land, because you are dependent on an intermediary
that is actively changing the deal. Channels where a summary cannot sit between
you and the reader — a newsletter, a video, a community — do not have that
property.

That is not an argument against doing SEO well. It is an argument against doing
only SEO.
